What printing is, and why it matters

Printing is a way of copying text and pictures. Ink, dye or toner is moved from a master, a plate or a digital file onto a surface.

That surface can be:

  • paper and cardboard
  • fabric and textiles
  • plastic
  • labels and packaging materials
  • other printable materials

Printing matters because it reproduces information, branding, designs and products in a physical form people can use. Every copy should look the same. That consistency is what your customers pay for.

Estimating and costing: looking forward, looking back

Estimating means predicting the resources, time and money a job will need, before production begins. The estimator uses the customer's specification to work out an expected cost.

An estimate may cover paper (also called stock), ink, machine hours, prepress, labour, finishing, delivery and other production needs. Prepress is the work of getting files and plates ready to print.

The main purpose is simple. The estimate tells you the right price to quote the customer.

Costing comes later. It tracks what the job really cost: the materials actually used, labour, machine time, overheads and other expenses. It answers one question: "How much did this job actually cost us?" You will study it properly in Chapter 14.

You need both. Estimating wins the work. Costing tells you whether the work was worth winning.

Why accurate estimating matters

The estimate sets the price you offer. So a mistake in either direction hurts.

Estimate too high

  • The customer may reject the quotation.
  • You may lose the job.
  • Over time, you become less competitive.

Estimate too low

  • You may win the job.
  • But the real cost turns out higher than expected.
  • Your profit shrinks or disappears.
The goalAccurate estimating = competitive pricing + cost control + potential profit

Accuracy is harder in print than in most trades, for four reasons:

  • Every job is different. A biscuit factory sells the same product all year. A printer sells a new product with every order, so each order needs its own cost.
  • Fixed costs come first. Plates, makeready and setup cost the same for 500 or 50,000 copies. Get the quantity wrong and the cost per copy is badly wrong.
  • Margins are thin. Commercial printers often keep only 5–10% of sales as net profit, after all costs. A 10% error in an estimate can wipe out the profit on the job.
  • Materials follow the dollar. Almost all paper, board, ink and plates used in Sri Lanka are imported. When the rupee weakens, your costs rise before your prices do.
Colombo shop floor

Three local habits make estimating harder here:

  • Customers often send artwork only after the price is agreed. So the estimator has to guess page counts and ink coverage.
  • Corporate and government work often comes with 60 to 90 days' credit. Your money is tied up long after the paper is paid for.
  • Many enquiries are a photo of someone else's job, with "same like this". That is not a specification.

What the estimator calculates

Every estimate is built from four groups of cost.

Materials

  • Paper quantity
  • Ink
  • Finishing materials, such as laminating film or glue
  • Spoilage: sheets wasted in setup and running

Labour

  • Prepress work
  • Running the press
  • Finishing
  • Other production work

Machine time

  • Setup time
  • Makeready
  • Run time
  • Number of impressions (one impression is one printing of a sheet)

Other costs

  • Freight: transport, such as bringing paper in
  • Delivery to the customer
  • Overheads: rent, power, office costs
  • Extra services, such as design or proofs

From enquiry to quote: ten steps

Steps 1 to 6 build an accurate estimate. Steps 7 to 10 turn it into a price, a quotation and a lesson for next time.

  1. Understand the jobFind out clearly what the customer wants. Log the enquiry with a number, the customer, the date and the deadline.
  2. Confirm the specificationCheck size, quantity, pages, colours, paper and finishing. Chapter 7 covers this in detail.
  3. Choose the printing methodPlan the production route: which press, which sheet size, how many copies fit on a sheet, how pages are arranged (the imposition) and what finishing. Two estimators can cost the same job very differently just by choosing different routes.
  4. Calculate the resourcesWork out materials, labour, machine time, prepress and finishing. Add allowances for waste, take times from your norms (your standard times) and use the hourly rate of each machine.
  5. Add the extra costsInclude delivery, proofing, freight and any other services.
  6. Review the estimateCheck the calculations and the specification again before any price goes out.
  7. Add the price elementsAdd profit, SSCL recovery and any risk allowance. This turns cost into price.
  8. Write the quotationRestate the specification, the price, how long the price is valid, the payment terms and VAT.
  9. Follow upCall or message the customer. A quote no one chases is wasted work.
  10. Compare estimate with actualWhen the job ticket comes back with the real times and paper used, check them against the estimate. This is how your norms get better.
Step 10 is the one most shops skip. Why does it matter so much?

Without it, your norms stay guesses and never improve. Say Ruwan always allows 0.5 hours for makeready, but the press crew really takes 0.8 hours. Then every four-colour quote understates press cost by about Rs 4,000. Only comparing estimate with actual will ever show that.

Estimate, price, quote, invoice

People use these four words as if they mean the same thing. They do not, and mixing them up is how shops lose money.

WordWhat it isWho sees it
EstimateA calculated prediction of what the job will cost to produce, based on the specification: paper, plates, press time, finishing, delivery.Only you and your staff
PriceThe estimate plus profit, plus any levy you must recover, such as SSCL. A business decision, not a calculation.Only you
Quotation (quote)The price presented to the customer for the specified job, with the exact specification, validity period, payment terms and VAT.The customer
InvoiceThe bill after delivery. It should match the quotation unless the specification changed.The customer and the tax authority

The estimate answers "What will this cost us?" The price answers "What should we charge?" Keep them separate.

If a customer pushes for a lower price, change the price, not the estimate. If your estimator edits the paper or press figures to make a price fit, you lose sight of the real cost. A year later, you cannot tell which kinds of work make money.

An estimate is based on the information and assumptions you have at the time. If the customer changes any of these six things, the price may change too:

  • quantity
  • paper
  • size
  • finishing
  • delivery
  • turnaround (how quickly they need the job)

A quote is only as accurate as the information behind it. Before you send one, run through these seven checks:

  1. Define the job requirements. What is being made, and what is it for?
  2. Confirm the quantity.
  3. Confirm the specifications: size, pages, colours and paper.
  4. Specify the finishing: laminating, folding, binding, cutting and so on.
  5. Confirm the artwork and prepress needs. Who supplies the files, and are they ready to print?
  6. Confirm the deadline and delivery.
  7. Review all costs before quoting.
Watch out

Do not quote a long validity period. Paper is priced in dollars, so a quotation valid for 90 days is a bet on the exchange rate. Most Colombo printers now quote 7 to 14 days for paper-heavy jobs. Others state that the price depends on the paper cost on the day of the order.

Kinds of estimate

  • Ballpark. A quick figure from experience. It tells a customer whether their budget is realistic. Never let it become the quotation.
  • Detailed estimate. The full calculation this course teaches. Use it for anything non-standard or large.
  • Repeat estimate. The last estimate, updated for today's paper prices, wage rates and exchange rate. First check that the specification really is the same.
  • Price list. Fixed prices for standard products, such as 100 business cards or an A2 poster. Common for digital and web-to-print (online ordering) work. Build the list from detailed estimates, and review it when costs move.
  • Tender. A formal bid, often for government or corporate work. It has sealed prices, strict specifications and penalty clauses. The capstone chapter covers it.

Who the estimator talks to

The estimator sits in the middle of the business. Information comes in from every side. The quote goes out to the customer.

If any of these links is weak, the estimate suffers. For example, if stores do not tell Ruwan the paper price went up last week, every quote this week is too cheap.

Skills of a good estimator

Technical skills

  • Print production knowledge: printing processes, materials, machines and production stages.
  • Material and cost awareness: how paper, ink, labour, machine time and overheads add up.
  • Maths: calculating quantities, costs, rates and production needs.
  • Software and MIS: estimating software, print management systems (MIS, a management information system) and Microsoft Office, especially Excel.

Soft skills

  • Communication: explain prices, requirements and problems clearly.
  • Negotiation: discuss prices and terms with customers and suppliers.
  • Problem solving: spot issues and find practical answers.
  • Attention to detail: a small specification error can change the final cost.
  • Time management: handle many estimates and deadlines at once.
  • Analytical thinking: understand the information and calculate accurately.
  • Teamwork: work with sales, production, purchasing and other teams.

A good estimator can picture the job on the shop floor, asks questions when the brief is vague, and keeps records. A great one also knows the market: roughly what competitors charge, which customers pay late and which jobs tie up the big press.

Owner's lens

Before you sign off a quote, and before you even look at the price, ask these five questions.

  • What production route did you choose, and why that press?
  • How many up (copies per sheet), and how many sheets including overs (extra sheets for waste)?
  • Which paper price did you use, and when did you last check it with the merchant?
  • Which hourly rates did you use, and when were they last updated?
  • What is the cost before profit, and what mark-up are we adding?

If the answers come quickly, the estimate was probably built properly. If not, it was probably built backwards from a price.

Where tax fits in

Two taxes touch every quotation in 2026. They work in opposite ways.

VAT: 18%

  • Added on top of your price and shown separately.
  • Not your money. You collect it for the government.
  • You deduct the VAT you paid on paper and other inputs.

SSCL: 2.5%

  • The Social Security Contribution Levy, charged on liable turnover.
  • A real cost to the business.
  • So you recover it through your price.
Worked exampleA printer counts as a manufacturer, so it pays SSCL on 85% of turnover.
SSCL = 2.5% × 85% = 2.125% of sales

Both registration thresholds fell to Rs 36 million a year in 2026. That pulled many small print shops into the system. The rate card has the details.

Remember

  • Estimating predicts cost before production; costing tracks the real cost afterwards.
  • An estimate is internal cost. A price adds profit. A quotation is the written offer to the customer.
  • Too high loses the job; too low loses the profit. High up-front costs and thin margins make small errors expensive.
  • Build the estimate in six steps, then price it, quote it, follow up and compare estimate with actual.
  • The estimator links the customer, the shop floor, the office and suppliers, and needs both technical and soft skills.
  • Imported materials and long credit terms make Sri Lankan estimating risky, so keep quotation validity short.
  • VAT is collected on top of the price. SSCL is a cost you build into it.

In one morning, a tea exporter in Kandy asks for 20,000 cartons and a bride in Negombo wants 100 invitations. You cannot cost either job until you know how it will be printed.

Why there are so many printing methods

No single printing method suits every job. Business cards, a brochure order, a T-shirt, a product label and a packaging box may all need different methods.

A printing method is simply a way of putting text, images or colour onto a surface. That surface might be paper, board, fabric, plastic, glass, metal, acrylic or packaging.

Seven factors decide which method fits. Each one comes with a question to ask before you choose.

FactorQuestion to askWhy it matters
ProductWhat product is being printed?It decides which production method is suitable.
QuantityHow many pieces are needed?Large and small quantities may need different methods.
MaterialWhat material will be used?Paper, fabric and plastic need different techniques.
Design detailIs the design simple or detailed?Fine detail may need a particular technology.
FinishDoes the print need a premium finish?Special finishes can change the process.
Turnaround timeHow quickly is the order needed?Urgent jobs may need a faster method.

Two numbers describe every method

For costing, you can reduce any printing method to two numbers:

  • Setup cost. What you spend before the first good copy: plates, cylinders, screens, makeready time and waste sheets.
  • Running cost per copy. Paper, ink or toner, and machine time for each copy after that.
FormulaTotal cost = setup cost + (running cost per copy × quantity)
Cost per copy = setup cost ÷ quantity + running cost per copy

Offset, flexo and gravure have a high setup. They get cheaper per copy the longer the run, because more copies share the setup.

Digital has very little setup. After the first few dozen copies, its cost per copy stays close to its running cost, whether you print 200 or 2,000.

The 15 printing methods

Here are all 15 methods, in three groups. For each one, note what it is best for and where you see it in Sri Lanka.

Main methods for paper, labels and packaging

Offset

Best for: long runs on paper
Setup: medium · Per copy: low
Sweet spot: 1,000 to 100,000

Ink goes from a plate to a rubber blanket, then onto the paper. Quality stays the same copy after copy.

Local example: brochures, catalogues, books, annual reports, letterheads and cartons. Colombo is full of second-hand Heidelberg, Komori and Mitsubishi presses in 52, 74 and 102 sizes.

Digital

Best for: short and personalised runs
Setup: very low · Per copy: high
Sweet spot: 1 to about 1,000

Toner or inkjet prints straight from the file, with no plates. Every sheet can be different, so names, numbers and QR codes are easy. It is costed mainly by the click charge per side, plus paper.

Local example: 100 wedding invitations or a small batch of business cards.

Flexographic

Best for: labels and packaging
Setup: medium to high · Per copy: low

Also called flexo. Flexible relief plates (the image stands up from the plate) print on a roll-fed press with fast-drying inks. Plates are made for each colour and reused on repeat orders. That is one reason brand owners stay with one packaging printer.

Local example: self-adhesive labels, wrappers, paper bags, cartons, plastic and flexible packaging, corrugated boxes.

Gravure

Best for: huge runs with fine detail
Setup: very high · Per copy: very low
Sweet spot: hundreds of thousands

The image is engraved into steel cylinders, one per colour. The cylinders are expensive but last for millions of impressions. They give superb, consistent colour, even on plastic film.

Local example: biscuit, snack, soap and tea packaging films; also magazines, decorative prints and high-volume labels.

Rotary

Best for: long, fast runs
Setup: high · Per copy: very low
Sweet spot: 50,000 and up

Its cylinders spin and print non-stop. It often prints on reels (big rolls) of paper instead of sheets. Web offset is the rotary press you will meet most. Paper waste at start-up is large, so it only pays on long runs.

Local example: newspapers, school exercise books and long-run magazines.

Inkjet

Best for: photos, posters, art prints, banners
Large format: setup very low · per m² medium

Tiny drops of ink are sprayed onto the surface. It is common for photos, posters, art prints and office printing. It shows colour detail well, but the result depends on ink quality, paper quality and printer settings.

Large-format (wide) inkjet printers use eco-solvent, solvent, UV or latex inks on flex, vinyl, fabric, canvas and board. In Sri Lanka this is mostly sold per square foot.

Local example: shop banners, hoardings, stage backdrops, pull-up stands and vehicle wraps.

Methods for special surfaces and products

Screen

Best for: fabric, promotional items, odd surfaces
Setup: low to medium · Per copy: medium
Sweet spot: short runs

Ink is pushed through a mesh screen (a stencil). It lays down thick, opaque ink on almost anything.

Local example: T-shirts, cotton bags, garment labels, posters, glass, plastic, metal nameplates and spot effects on print. It is big here because of the apparel industry.

UV

Best for: hard surfaces and premium gifts

Ultraviolet (UV) light dries, or cures, the ink almost instantly. So it can print on acrylic, glass and plastic.

Local example: acrylic awards, glass plaques, signs and premium corporate gifts.

Sublimation

Best for: mugs, polyester fabric, personalised gifts

Heat transfers dye directly into the material. The print becomes part of the surface.

Local example: photo mugs, polyester T-shirts and sports jerseys for school events.

Pad

Best for: small, curved or uneven items

A soft silicone pad picks up the design and presses it onto the product.

Local example: logos on pens, key tags, bottle caps and other promotional items.

3D printing

Best for: prototypes, samples, models

This is not normal printing. It puts no ink on a surface. Instead, it builds a solid object layer by layer. It belongs more to product development than to paper or packaging work.

Local example: a sample of a new display stand, or a model for an architect.

Methods that add a raised or shiny effect

Letterpress

Best for: premium stationery
Setup: low · Per copy: medium
Mostly finishing today

An old method that prints from raised type or blocks. It presses ink into the paper and leaves a slight texture. The same platen machines now do most of the foil stamping, embossing, numbering and die-cutting.

Local example: luxury wedding cards with a pressed feel.

Embossing

Best for: raised logos and titles

Not ink printing at all. It presses paper, card or packaging to make a raised design.

Local example: a raised logo on a tea carton or a company letterhead.

Foil stamping

Best for: metallic finishes

Heat and pressure lay down a thin metallic foil. Gold, silver and rose gold are common.

Local example: gold names on wedding cards and Avurudu gift boxes.

Thermography

Best for: raised, glossy text

Heat and a special powder create raised text or designs with a slightly glossy feel.

Local example: business cards and invitations.

Offset or digital?

For flat commercial work such as flyers and brochures, the choice is usually between these two.

Offset

  • Plates carry the image to a rubber blanket, then onto the paper.
  • More preparation and setup before printing starts.
  • Best for large volumes, the same design on every copy, and commercial printing.
  • Best when the setup cost can be spread over a large quantity.
  • Examples: 10,000 brochures, magazines, books, catalogues or large flyer orders.

Digital

  • Prints straight from the digital file, with no plate-making.
  • Quick setup, so it suits short runs and changes.
  • Best for small quantities, short turnaround and personalised printing.
  • Best when a long setup is not practical.
  • Examples: 100 invitations, personalised flyers, short-run brochures or small batches of business cards.

If both can meet the quality needed, pick the one with the lowest total cost at the ordered quantity. Run length decides most of it.

Offset costs much more to set up but much less per copy. The break-even point is where its saving per copy has paid back the extra setup. For this flyer, the lines cross near 750 copies.

Below 750, digital is cheaper. Above it, offset wins, and the gap grows fast.

Worked example: 5,000 flyersOffset = Rs 19,800 + (Rs 5.85 × 5,000) = about Rs 49,000
Digital = Rs 1,500 + (Rs 30.40 × 5,000) = about Rs 153,500
Offset costs roughly a third of digital.
The customer wants 600 flyers now and says they will "probably" need 3,000 more next month. What do you quote?

Quote both options. 600 on digital is cheaper today.

But 3,600 on offset in one run, with the extra 3,000 stored, costs far less than 600 on digital plus 3,000 on offset later with a second setup.

Give the customer both numbers. Let them decide whether storage and paying upfront are worth the saving. Also agree who owns the stored stock.

Other things that can change the choice

Run length is not the only factor. These can override it:

  • Material. Flex, fabric and PVC go to large format or screen. Plastic films go to flexo or gravure. Heavy board over about 400 gsm may not run on your digital press.
  • Variable data. Numbered tickets, personalised letters or unique QR codes need digital. The other route is offset followed by a separate numbering or inkjet step.
  • Colour match. A brand's special Pantone colour is easy on offset with a fifth printing unit. It is harder on a four-colour digital press.
  • Turnaround. Digital can deliver in hours. Offset needs plates, drying time (longer in Colombo's humidity) and cutting.
  • Size. A 20 × 10 ft stage backdrop can only be large format.

  • Tea cartons are usually sheetfed offset on duplex board, then die-cut and glued.
  • Paper swing tags for garments go to offset or digital. Woven and printed labels go to screen, flexo or thermal transfer.

What prepress does

Prepress is everything between receiving the customer's files and having plates or a digital print queue ready. It uses little machine time, but it hides a lot of people time.

A weak prepress check is where many expensive press mistakes begin. A problem found on screen costs minutes. Found on press, it costs paper, plates and hours.

The prepress flow

Most jobs pass through these six steps. Only jobs with foiling, embossing or die-cutting need the last one.

  1. Customer files arriveA manuscript, digital artwork, a PDF or an Illustrator file. Sometimes a Word document or a Canva link.
  2. File preparationPreflight the file first. Then fix problems, or do the design and typesetting if the customer asked. Then impose the pages on the press sheet.
  3. Digital proofShow the customer exactly what will print.
  4. Proof approvalThe customer checks the proof and signs it. No signature, no plates.
  5. Plate makingThe RIP (software that turns the file into printing dots) sends the pages to the CTP platesetter. A digital job goes to the digital press instead.
  6. Blocks and cutter preparationMetal blocks for foiling or embossing, and cutting dies for cartons, are made or ordered.

Preflight: the five key requirements

A design can look right on screen and still print badly. Preflight catches this. Check these five things on every file.

Colombo shop floor

Sinhala and Tamil text often causes trouble. Older files use legacy fonts such as FM Abhaya, which store Sinhala letters in the slots of Latin (English) letters. Newer files use Unicode fonts, the modern standard, such as Iskoola Pota or Noto Sans Sinhala.

If the PDF did not embed a font, or the font is missing at the RIP, the text can break. It may turn into English gibberish, or joined letters (conjuncts) may fall apart. The safe rule: ask for PDFs with all fonts embedded or outlined. For official and government work, always proof Sinhala and Tamil pages in hard copy.

Imposition and signatures

A book is not printed one page at a time. Several pages print together on one big sheet. The sheet is then folded into a signature: a section of pages in the right order.

A sheet printed on both sides and folded once gives 4 pages. Folded twice, it gives 8. Folded three times, it gives 16. Big sheets can make 32-page signatures.

Signatures matter to you because each one needs its own plates and makeready (press setup). So page count, sheet size and press size together decide the plate bill.

70 is not a multiple of 4, so it cannot be folded from whole sheets. On 8-page signatures it needs 8 full signatures plus 6 pages.

Proofing

There are four kinds of proof, from cheapest to dearest:

  • Soft proof. A PDF checked on screen. Free and fast, and good for text and layout. Useless for judging colour on an uncalibrated phone.
  • Digital hard proof. A colour-managed inkjet or digital print. Good for content and approximate colour. Costs a few hundred to a few thousand rupees a page.
  • Contract proof. A calibrated proof made to an agreed standard, such as ISO 12647. The pressman matches it on press. Use it for brand-critical work and packaging.
  • Wet proof or press proof. A real run on the press. Very expensive, and rarely done now except for large packaging jobs.

Plates and screening

Almost every Sri Lankan offset shop now uses CTP. Thermal or violet platesetters (plate-imaging machines) image aluminium plates straight from the RIP.

Many smaller printers do not own a platesetter. They buy plates from a plate bureau at a price per plate.

Either way, use one cost per plate. It should include the plate, the processing chemicals and the imaging time. This course uses Rs 800, Rs 1,500 and Rs 2,600 a plate for 52, 74 and 102 sizes.

Screening turns smooth tones into tiny dots. Commercial work is usually 150–175 lines per inch. Newspapers use around 100–120. Fine art books use more.

Screening rarely changes the cost. But finer screens need better paper and a well-maintained press.

Inside an offset press

Each printing unit has three main cylinders. The plate cylinder carries the plate. The blanket cylinder, covered in a rubber blanket, picks up the image from the plate. The impression cylinder presses the sheet against the blanket.

Rollers feed ink and water (dampening) to the plate.

The number of units decides how many colours print in one pass. A four-colour job on a one-colour press needs four passes per side. Each pass needs its own makeready, with drying time in between.

Makeready: the fixed part of press time

Makeready is everything from the last job's final sheet to this job's first good sheet. It takes about the same time for a short run as for a long one. The crew must:

  • wash up the blankets, and the ink rollers if colours change;
  • mount the plates;
  • set the feeder and delivery for the sheet size and paper thickness;
  • bring ink and water into balance;
  • get register (colours exactly on top of each other) and match colour to the proof;
  • get the OK sheet (the first approved good sheet) signed by the customer or supervisor.

On a modern four-colour 74 press, with CTP plates and a skilled crew, a simple makeready takes 20–40 minutes.

This course uses 0.5 hours per makeready. On a work-and-turn job (print one side, then turn the sheet to print the other), add 0.15 hours to turn the sheet.

Older presses without automatic plate loading, fussy brand colours and board all push makeready up.

Running: the variable part

Running time depends on the number of impressions and the speed. Speed is measured in IPH, impressions per hour.

Presses are rated at 12,000–15,000 sheets an hour. Short runs rarely get near that. Quality demands, the paper or board and humidity all slow the press down.

So plan with realistic speeds. Your own job tickets are the best source. Good planning speeds are:

Every pass has its own makeready. Every sheet goes through the press once per pass. So on a work-and-turn job, each sheet makes two impressions, one for each side.

FormulasNet sheets = quantity ÷ number up (copies per sheet)
Passes = colours on a side ÷ units on the press (rounded up), for each side
Impressions = sheets issued × passes
Press hours = (makereadies × makeready time) + (impressions ÷ running speed)
Press cost = press hours × machine hour rate

"Sheets issued" means net sheets plus the waste sheets explained under spoilage below.

Worked example5,000 A4 flyers, 4/4, 4 up, on Nawala's four-colour 74 press
Net sheets = 5,000 ÷ 4 = 1,250
Passes = 1 front + 1 back = 2
Sheets issued = 1,250 + makeready waste (100 × 2 = 200) + running waste (2% × 1,250 × 2 = 50) = 1,500
Impressions = 1,500 × 2 = 3,000
Press hours = (2 × 0.5) + (3,000 ÷ 8,000) = 1 + 0.375 = 1.375

Change "Printing units on press" to 2 in the calculator above. What happens to press time, and why?

Passes double from 2 to 4. So there are four makereadies instead of two, four lots of makeready waste, and more than twice the impressions. Press hours roughly double.

A two-colour press running four-colour work must also wait for the first two colours to dry before the next pass. That can mean a day's delay.

So a four-colour job quoted on a two-colour press must carry a bigger time and waste allowance, even if its hourly rate is lower.

Perfecting presses

A perfecting press (perfector) turns the sheet inside the press. It prints both sides in one pass, for example 4/4 on an 8-unit press.

It cuts handling time, but it usually has a higher hourly rate. Estimate one pass, with makeready for both sides.

Digital presses

Production digital presses in Colombo are mostly toner machines from Konica Minolta, Canon, Xerox and Ricoh. They run SRA3 sheets (320 × 450 mm). Some take up to 330 × 488 mm, or long banner sheets.

Their cost is built in three parts:

  • Clicks. The service contract charges a fee for each printed side, called a click. It covers toner, parts and the engineer. Colour clicks cost several times more than black. The course uses Rs 24 per SRA3 colour side.
  • Machine time. An hourly rate for the operator, space, power and depreciation. Apply it to setup and running time.
  • Paper. Digital-certified stocks cost a little more. Humid storage causes jams and curl.
FormulaDigital cost = (SRA3 sheets × sides × click charge) + (sheets × paper cost) + (hours × machine rate)

Large format, flexo and gravure

Large format

Costed by: square metre or square foot

Speed depends on print mode. An eco-solvent printer might do 20–25 m² an hour in draft mode, but only 10–14 m² in the quality mode most customers want.

Cost = media + ink per m² + machine time + finishing, such as hemming, eyelets, pole pockets or mounting on board. Chapter 12 costs a banner in full.

Flexo

Costed by: plates, metres and substrate

Flexo label presses print from a reel (the web). They print several labels across it and many along it.

Setup means a plate per colour, choosing the anilox (ink-metering) roller and threading the web. Speed is in metres per minute. Start-up waste is in metres.

Key numbers: plate cost per colour, repeat length (one print repeat along the reel), labels across, running metres and substrate (label material) cost per m².

Gravure

Costed by: cylinders as tooling, then long runs

Gravure uses an engraved steel cylinder for each colour. The cylinders are a large one-off cost, charged to the customer as tooling.

They last for millions of impressions. So gravure only makes sense for products the customer will reorder many times.

Spoilage on press

Some sheets are always lost. There are makeready sheets and sheets pulled for checking. There are hickeys (small spots from dust or dried ink), marks during the run, and feeding faults.

The course allows 100 sheets per makeready, plus 2% of net sheets per pass for running waste. Chapter 11 covers allowances in full.

Colombo shop floor

Humidity above 70% is normal in Colombo for much of the year. Paper that has not settled to the pressroom air curls and misregisters (colours fall out of line). Ink dries slowly.

Heavy solids set off onto the back of the next sheet, unless spray powder is used or the stack is kept low. Shops with air-conditioned pressrooms run faster with less waste. If yours does not, your norms should say so.

Owner's lens

Ask your press crew to write the actual makeready and running times on every job ticket for a month. Compare them with the 0.5 hour and 8,000 sheets an hour in your estimates.

If makereadies average 50 minutes, your press cost is too low on every short run. And short runs are most of what Colombo printers do.

Cutting

The guillotine cuts a stack of sheets, called a lift, in one stroke. A lift holds roughly 600–800 sheets of 130 gsm art paper. (Gsm, grams per square metre, is the paper's weight.) Board gives fewer sheets per lift.

So cutting time depends on the number of lifts and the cuts per lift. It does not simply follow the number of sheets.

FormulaGuillotine hours = setup + (lifts × cuts per lift) ÷ cuts per hour

A 2 × 2 layout with bleed needs cuts all round, plus a double cut in each gutter (the gap between pieces). That is eight or more cuts per lift.

A guillotine manages around 120 cuts an hour, including jogging (knocking the stack square) and handling. So a few thousand sheets can easily take an hour.

Folding

A buckle folder pushes the sheet into a plate until it buckles through rollers. A knife folder uses a blade, for cross folds.

Folders are fast: around 5,000 sheets an hour on commercial work. But each new fold pattern needs 15–30 minutes of setup.

Paper heavier than about 170 gsm should be creased before folding. If not, it cracks along the fold, especially through the dark solid colours customers love.

Surface finishing

Converting: dies for cartons

Cartons and folders are cut and creased to shape by a die, also called a cutting forme. It is made of steel rules bent to the design and set in plywood.

A die for a small carton layout can cost tens of thousands of rupees. It is kept for repeat orders.

After die-cutting, the waste between the cartons is stripped away by hand or by a stripping unit. Then the cartons are folded and glued on a folder-gluer.

Owner's lens

Outsourced finishing

Few Colombo printers own every finishing machine. Foiling, spot UV, case binding, section sewing and sometimes lamination go to trade finishers (specialist finishing firms).

On the estimate, this becomes a direct expense: a cost bought in for this one job. It is made up of:

  • the finisher's quote;
  • transport both ways;
  • extra time in the schedule;
  • a handling margin, to cover your risk if they spoil sheets.

Packing, dispatch and delivery

Packing is labour plus materials: kraft wrap, shrink film, cartons and labels. It is easy to forget, but it is a real cost.

Then the packed job goes out through dispatch:

  1. Dispatch departmentReceives the packed job and gets it ready to go out.
  2. Delivery note and invoiceThe delivery note lists what is sent, and the customer signs it. The invoice asks for payment.
  3. Delivery or collectionYour van or a courier takes the job to the customer, or the customer collects it.

Delivery is a trip cost for your van, or a courier charge. Say in the quotation where delivery is included, for example "delivered to one address within Colombo city limits".

Watch out

Finishing needs its own waste allowance. Die-cutting, lamination and binding all spoil a few percent of sheets.

If the press printed only the net quantity plus press waste, the finished count will come up short.

Owner's lens

When you look at a quote, check finishing's share of the total. For a leaflet it is typically 5–10%. For a perfect-bound report it is often 8–15%. For cartons it is 15–20%, once die-cutting, stripping, gluing and packing are included.

If the share looks low, it probably is.

  • The post-press flow: finishing processes, then final cutting, then counting and packing.
  • Finishing covers cutting, folding, binding, surface finishing, converting and packing.
  • Guillotine time follows lifts × cuts per lift. Saddle stitching suits thin booklets.

Combining work to share setup

Plates and makeready cost the same for a small run or a big one. So share them whenever you can.

  • Gang runs. Several customers' jobs share one sheet and one set of plates. It is common for business cards, postcards and stickers: 21 different cards fit on an SRA3 sheet. This is how online printers sell cheap cards.
  • Combination runs. Different items for one customer, printed together, such as a letterhead and compliment slip on the same paper and colours.
  • Run-ons. Extra copies printed during the same run. They carry no setup cost, so their price per 1,000 is far lower. Always quote a run-on rate, because it often persuades the customer to order more.
  • Split runs. One design with a small change, such as a different branch address. Only the black plate changes.
A customer orders 1,000 letterheads, 1,000 compliment slips and 1,000 business cards, all 4/0. How could one method of working save money?

In-house or bought in?

Every printer buys in some work. Examples are foiling, case binding, a colour your press cannot print, or extra capacity when your presses are full.

Buying in is also called trade printing or outsourcing. Base the decision on relevant costs: only the costs that really change if you do the work.

Your machine is idle

  • Its fixed costs are paid anyway.
  • The only extra cost in-house is the variable cost: materials, power and consumables.
  • So in-house almost always wins.

Your machine is fully booked

  • Doing the job in-house delays other work.
  • Compare the outside price with your full in-house cost, plus the profit you would lose on the job you push out.

Either way, also allow for transport both ways, extra time in the schedule, and the quality risk you no longer control.

Four properties that change the estimate

Step 4. Ream weight = sheet weight × 500 = 0.07545 × 500 = 37.73 kg
Step 5. Price per sheet = sheet weight × price per kg = 0.07545 × Rs 460 = Rs 34.707, about Rs 34.71
Step 6. Price per ream = price per sheet × 500 = Rs 34.707 × 500 = Rs 17,354

Tip: keep the decimals until the last step. If you round too early, the answer can move by a few rupees.

A book's spine must fit its pages. Each leaf carries two pages, so divide the pages by 2 to get the leaves.

Worked exampleA 64-page report on 115 gsm matt art (about 0.095 mm per leaf), with a 300 gsm cover (about 0.33 mm)

Colombo shop floor

Nearly all printing paper and board in Sri Lanka is imported, mainly from Indonesia, China, India and Thailand, and priced off the US dollar. A 5% slide in the rupee lifts paper costs about 5% within a few shipments.

Humidity is the other local enemy. Paper unwrapped straight into a hot, humid pressroom takes on moisture at the edges. It curls, gets wavy edges and misregisters (the colours stop lining up). Keep reams wrapped, on pallets off the floor, and let them stand in the pressroom for a day before printing.

Garment brands selling in Europe often demand FSC-certified paper for swing tags and packaging. That narrows supply and raises the price.

Paper is 35–65% of cost on most jobs. So a stale paper price in the estimating sheet is the most expensive mistake you can make.

Process colours and spot colours

Process colours (CMYK)

  • Cyan, magenta, yellow and black.
  • Printed as tiny dots over one another.
  • Reproduce photographs and most colours.

Spot colours

  • Ink mixed to one exact shade, usually a Pantone reference.
  • Used for brand colours, metallics and fluorescents.

Spot colours cost more in three ways:

  1. Dearer ink. The ink itself costs more per kg.
  2. An extra plate and unit. You need a fifth plate and printing unit, or an extra pass through the press.
  3. Leftover waste. Mixed ink left at the end of the job is often thrown away, unless the same colour is ordered again soon.

On small jobs, the leftover can be more ink than the job actually used.

How much ink a job uses

Ink use depends on four things. They are the number of impressions, the printed area, the coverage, and the thickness of the ink film.

FormulaInk (grams) = impressions × printed area per impression (m²) × total coverage × ink film at 100% cover (g/m²)
Then add a waste allowance for the duct, rollers and wash-up.
  • Coverage. A text page may be 5–8% black. A photo-heavy brochure page averages 15–25% per colour. A carton with a solid background can be 50–100% on one colour. Total coverage is all the colours added together.
  • Ink film (mileage). Offset on coated paper needs about 1.2–1.4 g/m². Uncoated paper absorbs more, so it needs about 1.5–1.8 g/m². This course uses 1.3.
  • Waste. Some ink stays in the duct and on the rollers. Allow 10% on commercial jobs, and more on short runs and spot colours.

Ink coverage is one of the nine specifications in Chapter 7. Ask for it, or judge it from the artwork.

Coatings and varnishes

Varnishes and coatings protect the print. They also add a gloss or matt look.

  • Oil-based overprint varnish is applied from a printing unit, just like an ink.
  • Aqueous (water-based) coating is applied by a coater unit at the end of the press. It dries fast.
  • UV varnish is applied in-line or offline, and cured (hardened) by UV lamps.

Estimate a coating like ink: area × coverage × film weight, at the coating's price per kg. Aqueous coating typically goes on at about 2–4 g/m² wet.

  • Flexo and gravure ink is costed by volume. That depends on the anilox or cylinder cell volume (how much ink the tiny cells hold), the printed area and the run length in metres. Packaging converters build these into their own tables.

What a norm is

A norm is a planning figure for a normal job. It assumes a trained operator on a well-maintained machine. For example:

  • "Makeready on the 74 press takes 0.5 hours."
  • "Allow 2% running waste per pass."
  • "The saddle stitcher does 1,500 books an hour."

Good norms make estimates fast and consistent. Bad norms make every estimate wrong in the same direction.

IPH: the basic speed norm

The most basic norm is speed. For a press, speed is counted in IPH, impressions per hour.

An impression is one sheet side going through the press. A four-colour press prints all four colours on one side in a single pass, so that counts as one impression.

Worked exampleRunning time = impressions ÷ IPH
Nawala's flyer job needs 2,826 impressions on the 74 press
The running norm is 8,000 sheets an hour, so 8,000 IPH
Running time = 2,826 ÷ 8,000 = 0.35 hours, about 21 minutes
Makeready time is added on top.

Manufacturers rate their presses at higher speeds. Short runs rarely get there, so use your own realistic IPH.

Where norms come from

Norms come from four places. The last one is the best.

  1. Manufacturer figures. Rated speeds and setup times. They are always optimistic, so treat them as a ceiling.
  2. Industry tables. Printing trade associations and training bodies publish typical allowances. They are useful as a starting point.
  3. Work study. Timing a sample of real jobs, with allowances for rest and interruptions.
  4. Your own job tickets. The best source. Average the actual times and waste recorded over the last few months, job type by job type.

Overs: the spoilage allowance

You never get out exactly the number of sheets you feed in. Sheets are lost at makeready, during the run and in every finishing process.

The extra sheets you issue to cover this are called overs. They have three parts.

FormulaOvers = (makereadies × waste per makeready) + (net sheets × running % × passes) + (net sheets × finishing %)
Worked exampleNawala's flyer job: 1,250 net sheets, one makeready, 2 passes, 2% running waste per pass, 1% finishing waste
Makeready waste = 1 × 100 = 100
Running waste = 1,250 × 2% × 2 = 50
Finishing waste = 1,250 × 1% = 12.5
Overs = 100 + 50 + 12.5 = 162.5, round up to 163 sheets
Sheets to issue = 1,250 + 163 = 1,413

Two patterns matter:

Short runs

  • The fixed makeready waste dominates.
  • 100 waste sheets on a 500-sheet job is 20%.

Long runs

  • The percentages dominate.
  • The same 100 sheets is only 1% of a 10,000-sheet run.
  • But 1% either way can mean thousands of sheets.

The customer insists on "exactly 5,000, not one less." What does that mean for overs?

You must issue enough overs that, even on a bad day, at least 5,000 good copies come out. That means a larger allowance, and the extra copies are yours to absorb.

Many printers add a delivery tolerance to their terms. It is commonly ±5% for commercial work and ±10% for packaging, charged pro rata (for the copies actually delivered). If the customer refuses a tolerance, cost the higher overs into the job.

Quality standards also cost money

"Standards" in print also means quality standards. These affect cost too.

  • ISO 12647-2 sets targets for offset colour, such as ink density, dot gain (dots printing larger than planned) and grey balance. Printing to it needs calibrated plates and proofs, and more careful makereadies. Brand-owner packaging often requires it.
  • ISO 9001 is a quality management system. It adds paperwork and inspection time, but reduces reprints.
  • FSC and PEFC chain-of-custody certification lets you label products as coming from responsibly managed forests. Garment exporters' tags and boxes often need it.
  • Customer specifications, such as a pharmaceutical client's carton standard, can require 100% inspection. That is a real labour cost.

Build norms from your own records

  1. RecordEvery job ticket records actual makeready time, running time, sheets issued and sheets delivered.
  2. GroupEach month, group jobs by type (4-colour leaflets, books, cartons) and by machine.
  3. AverageWork out the average and the spread. If makeready averages 42 minutes, with most jobs between 30 and 55, use about 0.7 hours.
  4. Check odd resultsInvestigate outliers before you discard them. A 3-hour makeready is either a recording error or a problem worth fixing.
  5. UpdateUpdate the estimating norms every quarter.

The common market price is Rs 120 per sq ft. So the banner sells for Rs 4,800. That is a gross margin of about 35% before SSCL.

Try it: change the banner size or your selling price per square foot, and see how the cost per square foot and the margin change.

Watch out

Do not price large format only by the square foot of the finished banner. A 3 × 2 ft banner needs the same setup and file-check time as a 10 × 4 ft one.

That fixed time is spread over only 6 sq ft, so the cost per square foot is much higher. Set a minimum charge per job.

A job is complex when it has one or more of these:

Looking forward, looking back

What is costing?

Costing is calculating or tracking the actual expenses of producing a printing job.

It answers one plain question: How much did this job actually cost us?

To answer it, costing looks at five kinds of cost.

  • Materials actually used: paper, board, plates, ink.
  • Labour involved: the hours people worked on the job.
  • Machine usage: the hours each machine ran.
  • Overheads: shared costs such as rent and power.
  • Other production expenses: outwork, transport.

Costing leads step by step to profit.

  1. Actual production costsRecord what the job really used and spent.
  2. Financial controlSpot waste and overruns, and keep spending within plan.
  3. ProfitabilityKnow which jobs and customers make money, and price better next time.

Post-costing: the sixth stage of every job

In Chapter 2 you met the six stages of the printing process. The last stage is post-costing. It happens after the job is delivered.

  1. Actual production costsCollect what the job really used: paper, plates, press hours, prepress hours, outwork.
  2. Compare with estimated costsPut the actual figures next to the estimate, line by line.
  3. Review the jobAsk why each line went over or under. Is it a norm to fix, or a charge to bill?
  4. Collect the final paymentInvoice any agreed extras and collect the balance.

Techniques of costing

A technique is a way of treating costs. You can use any technique with any method.

  • Historical costing: recording costs after they are incurred.
  • Standard costing: setting planned costs for each operation (your norms and rates). You then study the differences from actual, called variances.
  • Absorption costing: charging all production costs, fixed and variable, to jobs. Machine hour rates are absorption costing.
  • Marginal costing: charging only variable costs to jobs. Fixed costs are treated as a cost of the period. It is used for short-term decisions.
  • Uniform costing: several firms in one industry use the same costing rules, so their results can be compared. Printing trade federations in Britain and America were among the first to publish uniform costing systems, in the early twentieth century.

Behaviour: the grouping owners use most

  • Fixed costs stay the same in total over a period, whatever the output: rent, depreciation, salaries, insurance.
  • Variable costs rise in proportion to output: paper, plates, ink, outwork, clicks.
  • Semi-variable costs have both parts: electricity (a fixed charge plus units used), telephone, a delivery van (insurance plus fuel).
  • Stepped costs are fixed over a range, then jump. One press crew handles up to one shift of work. A second shift needs a second crew.

Normal and abnormal

Normal spoilage is expected waste, such as makeready waste within the norm. It is part of the cost of good output, so it is charged to jobs.

Abnormal loss is unexpected. For example, a whole pallet of paper is ruined by a leaking roof. It is not a cost of any job.

So you write it off to the profit and loss account. That way it does not distort job costs.

Contribution and break-even

Once you split costs into fixed and variable, a powerful idea follows.

Each rupee of sales first pays its own variable costs. What is left is its contribution towards fixed costs.

When contributions add up to the fixed costs, the business breaks even. Everything after that is profit.

FormulasContribution = sales − variable costs
Contribution ratio = contribution ÷ sales
Break-even sales = fixed costs ÷ contribution ratio
Sales for a target profit = (fixed costs + target profit) ÷ contribution ratio
Margin of safety = (actual sales − break-even sales) ÷ actual sales

In a slow month, a customer offers Rs 40,000 for a job Ruwan estimates at Rs 48,000 full cost. The variable costs (paper, plates, ink, power, outwork) are Rs 31,000. Should Nawala take it?

If the press would otherwise stand idle, probably yes. Fixed costs are being paid anyway. So the job adds Rs 40,000 − Rs 31,000 = Rs 9,000 of contribution towards them.

But check three things first:

  • It will not push out full-price work.
  • It does not set a low price for this customer's future orders.
  • Other customers will not hear about it.

This is marginal costing in action. For short-term decisions, fixed costs that do not change are irrelevant.

Owner's lens

Know your monthly break-even sales figure by heart. Update it when rent, wages or the paper-to-sales ratio changes.

On the 20th of each month, compare sales to date with it. That single number tells you whether to push sales, take marginal work or hold prices.

Remember

  • Printing mainly uses job costing. Batch, contract, operating and multiple costing each have a place.
  • Techniques include historical, standard, absorption, marginal and uniform costing.
  • Costs can be classified by nature, function, traceability, behaviour, controllability, normality, time and relevance.
  • Fixed, variable and semi-variable behaviour underpins break-even: fixed costs ÷ contribution ratio.
  • For short-term decisions, a price above variable cost adds contribution. Abnormal losses are written off, not charged to jobs.

What a costing system is

A costing system is a set of forms, records, rules and people. Together they turn daily activity into cost information.

How cost information flows

Four kinds of record feed each job's cost card. The card is then used in two checks.

Stores requisitions bring in materials. Time sheets bring in labour and machine hours. Outwork invoices bring in direct expenses. Overhead rates add each job's share of overheads.

Recording time: keeping and booking

Labour time is the record most often done badly. You need two different kinds of time record.

Time-keeping

  • Records attendance: when each person arrives and leaves
  • Used for payroll
  • Kept in the attendance register or clock-in system

Time-booking

  • Records the work done on each job
  • Used for costing
  • Kept on the time sheet or job ticket at the machine

You need both, and they should agree. Attendance time = time booked to jobs + idle time + other time.

Idle time is paid time with no job to work on, such as waiting for plates or a proof approval. Book time at the machine, as the work happens.

Nimali's press crew attended for 48 hours this week, but only 39 hours are booked to jobs. Is that a problem?

Not always. Attendance time = booked time + idle time + other time. So the missing 9 hours must be explained.

Some may be idle time, such as waiting for paper or approval. Some may be cleaning, maintenance or training.

If nobody can explain the hours, work is not being booked to jobs. Then job costs look lower than they really are, and future estimates will be too low.

Reconciling cost and financial accounts

You may keep the cost accounts and the financial accounts separately. If so, their profit figures will differ. The reasons are predictable:

  • Items only in the financial accounts: interest earned on a fixed deposit, profit or loss on selling a machine, fines and penalties, donations, income tax.
  • Items only in the cost accounts: notional charges. These are charges the business does not actually pay, such as interest on the owner's capital, or rent for premises the owner owns.
  • Over- or under-absorbed overheads (Chapter 23).
  • Different stock values, for example if cost records price paper differently from the year-end valuation.

A reconciliation statement starts with the cost profit. It adds and subtracts each difference. It should arrive exactly at the financial profit.

If it does not, something is missing from one set of records.

The seven elements

Ask one question about every cost: can you trace it to one job? If yes, it is a direct cost. If no, it is an overhead.

Is it direct or not?

  • A cutting die for one carton order is a direct expense. It is made for one job, but it does not become part of the carton.
  • Blanket wash and press rags are production overheads (indirect materials). They are used on every job and cannot sensibly be traced to one.
  • The sales representative's commission is a selling and distribution overhead, because it is a cost of winning orders.

Direct materials. A plate set is made for one job, so you can count it and charge it exactly. Blanket wash is used on all jobs, so it is a production overhead.

The test is whether you can trace the cost to a job at a sensible effort. Ink is traced when it is weighed or calculated per job. A busy shop printing mostly black text may treat small amounts of ink as overhead.

What material control aims for

  • The right material, of the right quality, in the right quantity, at the right time and the right price.
  • No production held up for lack of material.
  • No more cash tied up in stock than necessary.
  • Protection against theft, damage, deterioration (going bad) and obsolescence (going out of use).
  • Accurate records, so that stock values and job costs are right.

The basics of a good store

  • Coding. Every stock item gets a unique code, for example ART-G-130-2536 for 130 gsm gloss art, 25 × 36 in. Without codes, one paper appears under three names and the records fall apart.
  • Standard specifications. Write down exactly what you buy, so a cheaper substitute is not accepted by mistake.
  • Proper storage. Keep paper off the floor on pallets, wrapped, and away from walls and roof leaks. Put the oldest stock in front.
  • Authorised issues. Nothing leaves the store without a requisition (a written request) carrying a job number.
  • Records. Keep bin cards in the store and a stores ledger in the office, and check one against the other.

Economic order quantity (EOQ)

Small, frequent orders mean many orders. Each one has paperwork, bank charges and clearing costs.

  • Bin card: kept where the stock sits. It shows quantity received, issued and balance, with no rupee values.

In a small shop, the owner or production manager often buys everything. As the business grows, you choose between two ways of buying.

The purchase procedure, step by step

  1. Purchase requisitionThe storekeeper raises one when stock reaches the re-order level. Production raises one for a special paper for one job, with the job number. It states item code, quantity and date needed, and an authorised person approves it.
  2. Choose the supplierFor regular items, use your approved list. For new or large purchases, send enquiries and compare quotations on price, quality, delivery time, credit terms and reliability. Never on price alone.
  3. Purchase orderSend a numbered order with the exact specification, quantity, price, delivery date and address, and terms. Copies go to stores, to expect the delivery, and to accounts, to check the invoice later.
  4. Follow upChase orders close to their due date, especially paper for booked jobs.
  5. Receive and inspectStores counts what arrived and checks it against the PO. For paper: count reams, check labels for gsm, size and grain, weigh a sample sheet, and look for water damage. Stores then raises a GRN for what was accepted.
  6. Return faulty goodsWrong or damaged goods go back with a material returned note. The supplier then issues a credit note.
  7. Check the invoice: the three-way matchAccounts compares the PO, the GRN and the supplier's invoice. The invoice is approved only if all three agree.
  8. Record and payEnter the invoice in the stores ledger at cost and pay on the agreed terms. If you are VAT registered, record the VAT as input tax (VAT you can claim back).

Which price for an estimate?

On 25 September Ruwan estimates a new job needing 10 reams. Which price should he use: FIFO (Rs 18,000), weighted average (Rs 17,600) or the merchant's new quote of Rs 18,600?

The merchant's current price, Rs 18,600. An estimate looks forward. Whatever paper this job uses will have to be replaced at today's price.

Book prices from FIFO or weighted average are for valuing stock and recording past job costs. If you use them in estimates when prices are rising, every quote understates the cost.

  • Standard price variances. Say Nawala sets a standard of Rs 17,500 a ream. The 18 Sep purchase at Rs 18,000 then gives a price variance.

Incentive schemes

Paying for output rather than time can raise productivity. But it works only if standards are fair and quality is protected.

  • Time rate: paid for hours, whatever the output. Simple, but no incentive.
  • Piece rate: paid per unit, such as per bill book made. A strong incentive, but it needs quality checks.
  • Halsey plan: time rate for hours worked, plus a share (commonly 50%) of the time saved against a standard.
  • Rowan plan: time rate plus a bonus of (time saved ÷ time allowed) × time taken × rate.
  • Group bonus: a press crew or finishing team shares a bonus. This suits work done by teams.
Worked exampleStandard time 10 h, actual time 8 h, so 2 h saved. Rate Rs 500 an hour.
Time rate: 8 × 500 = Rs 4,000
Halsey: 4,000 + 50% × 2 × 500 = Rs 4,500
Rowan: 4,000 + (2 ÷ 10) × 8 × 500 = Rs 4,800
At standard time the job would cost 10 × 500 = Rs 5,000

The employer still saves under both plans, and the worker earns more than the time rate.

Try it: change the standard time, actual time or rate and compare what each scheme pays.

Why does the Rowan bonus stop growing as time saved approaches the whole standard?

The Rowan bonus is (saved ÷ allowed) × taken × rate. When the worker saves more time, they also work fewer hours. So the two parts pull against each other.

The bonus peaks when the job is done in half the standard time. That protects the employer from paying out on a standard that was set too loosely.

Owner's lens

Six steps from invoice to job

  1. CollectWhen you record an overhead invoice or payroll item, code it to an overhead account, such as rent or electricity.
  2. ClassifyGroup the codes by function (production, office, selling, delivery) and by behaviour (fixed or variable).

  6. Cost of salesCost of goods sold + selling and distribution overheads.
  7. SalesCost of sales + profit.

Why the adjustments? You add opening work in progress, because this month finished those jobs. You deduct closing work in progress, because that cost belongs to jobs finished next month. Finished jobs not yet delivered are treated the same way.

Mark-up and margin

Mark-up is profit as a percentage of cost. Margin is profit as a percentage of the selling price.

They are not the same number. A 20% mark-up is only a 16.7% margin.

FormulasMargin = mark-up ÷ (1 + mark-up)
Mark-up = margin ÷ (1 − margin)
Worked example20% mark-up: 0.20 ÷ 1.20 = 16.7% margin
25% mark-up: 0.25 ÷ 1.25 = 20% margin
To earn a 25% margin: 0.25 ÷ 0.75 = 33.3% mark-up
Watch out

Mixing these up is one of the commonest pricing errors in small businesses. An owner wants a 25% margin and tells staff to add 25% to cost. The shop earns only 20%.

Try it: enter a cost and a mark-up, and read the margin you really earn.

Next month Nawala's sales fall to Rs 16 million, but fixed overheads stay the same. What happens to the ratios, and what is the risk?

Factory, administration and selling overheads are mostly fixed. On a smaller volume of work, they become a bigger percentage.

If Ruwan keeps using September's percentages, quotes will under-recover overhead. If he switches to the new, higher percentages, quotes rise just when the market is slow, and more work may be lost.

The answer: set overhead rates on a normal level of activity. Treat a slow month's shortfall as under-absorption (Chapter 23), not as a reason to change prices.

Step 1: Read the conditions

Your task: find every condition in the notice, not just the specification, that changes your price.

Which conditions change the price, and how?
  1. 90-day validity. You carry the risk of paper prices and the exchange rate moving for three months, plus production time. So add a risk allowance on paper.
  2. Payment 45 days after delivery. Add production time, and your money is out for roughly 75 days. So add a finance cost at your borrowing rate.
  3. Liquidated damages. At 0.5% a week, that is about Rs 5,800 a week on this contract. Three weeks is tight for perfect binding plus lamination if the proof round slips. So plan the schedule with a buffer.
  4. Bid security. Cash or a bank guarantee is tied up until the award. A small cost, but a real one.
  5. "Lowest evaluated responsive bid". A bid that misses a required document is rejected, whatever its price. So compliance comes first.

Step 2: Clarify before you calculate

Your task: write the questions you would send the bank before the pre-bid deadline.

What would you ask the bank, and why?
  1. Is delivery to one address only, or will regional branches get copies? This changes packing and delivery.
  2. How many rounds of corrections are included, and what if artwork arrives late? This protects your 21 days and the liquidated damages clause.
  3. Is the roll-up stand economy or premium? The stand is most of Item C's cost.
  4. Is a tolerance of ±5% on quantities acceptable? See Chapter 11.

Ask through the formal clarification process. The bank answers all bidders. That also protects you from a competitor who assumes the cheapest reading.

Owner's lens

Before you sign any tender, ask three questions:

  1. What is our cost if paper rises 5% before we buy?
  2. What does it cost us if we deliver two weeks late?
  3. After both, is the profit left still worth the bid security and the credit period?

If the answer to the last one is no, the right bid may be no bid.
